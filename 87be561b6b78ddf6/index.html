Anduin

Rating providers for AI agents from the payments they already make

Software programs that act on their own, called AI agents, now buy services from one another: data, computation, another agent’s work. They pay for each request as it happens, often a cent at a time, in digital dollars called stablecoins, sent over onchain payment rails such as x402 and recorded on public ledgers called blockchains. Anyone can list a service in a marketplace such as x402 Bazaar, so an agent choosing a new provider cannot tell a good one from a fraud.

Every proposed fix adds a burden to the market: a gatekeeper who approves providers, identity papers, a paid referee who checks each purchase for more than the purchase costs, or reviews that nobody writes. Anduin adds none of these. It rates providers using nothing but the payments already on the ledger, and it makes a rating cost real money to fake. This page walks through the idea and the paper’s results.

The market

AI agents buy services from providers and pay per request: a few cents in stablecoins, settled on a blockchain. Anyone can sell, and anyone can buy.

One agent, the buyer, needs a service it has never bought. It faces a list of strangers, with no brands, no reviews and no one to complain to. Which stranger can it trust?

What the market does have is its ledger. A payment on a blockchain is public and permanent by nature, so anyone can see every payment ever made: who paid whom, and how much.

Each line here is one buyer’s payments to one provider. That record is all Anduin reads.

Drawing the market

Buyers pay providers, never each other, and providers never pay providers. So think of the participants as nodes and the payments as edges between them: buyers in one column, providers in the other, every edge crossing between. The paper calls this a bipartite graph.

Each edge adds up one buyer’s payments to one provider over the last 90 days, fees included: its weight. Thicker edges carry more dollars.

This graph is the whole input. A rating is any rule that reads it and scores a provider for one buyer.

Scoring a stranger

Take the buyer. Over many small payments it has paid two providers $4 and $2 in total: its known providers.

Two other buyers pay the first known provider too, $8 and $1. The paper calls them co-users; here, shared customers.

The shared customers also pay a provider the buyer never has: the candidate. $8 from one, $1 from the other.

The rest of the market plays no part in this buyer’s rating, so it fades.

A buyer that pays the same provider as you probably needs the same things, so where it spends next is evidence. Trust runs along a path: buyer, known provider, shared customer, candidate.

A path carries no more trust than its thinnest edge. Through the first shared customer, the path carries $4. Through the second, $1.

The attack

One provider wants to be every buyer’s first pick: the attacker. Joining the market is free, so it creates fake buyers under its own control.

One party posing as many is the Sybil attack, named in 2002 for the file-sharing networks of the early internet.

A payment from a fake to the attacker costs only the network fee, about two cents, because the money comes straight back. A payment to a real provider is gone for good.

That difference is the whole defence.

Three simple ratings, and how each one falls.

Usage ranking counts a provider’s buyers, as marketplaces do today. The fakes pay the attacker itself, so any number of buyers costs one fee each.

Co-user count counts the candidate’s shared customers, the rule behind “customers who bought this also bought”, known as collaborative filtering. Each fake pays a known provider one cent to qualify as a shared customer, then pays the attacker. Two fees per fake.

Co-user volume adds up what the shared customers spend at the candidate. One fake pays a known provider a cent, then pays the attacker a fortune of the attacker’s own money. A couple of fees.

All three take the attacker’s fabricated edges at face value. Each falls for transaction fees alone.

Anduin

Anduin’s rule: trust reaches a candidate only along paths that start at the buyer’s own payments, and a path carries no more than its thinnest edge.

A fake that paid a known provider one cent carries one cent, however much it pays the attacker. The attacker scores three cents.

Splitting the same money across more fakes buys nothing. Six fakes at half a cent each still carry three cents.

Now make the rule exact. Treat trust as a quantity that flows, so the buyer’s $4 edge cannot be used twice: both paths share it, and the candidate scores $4. However rich the attacker, no candidate can score more than the buyer itself has spent.

The paper calls this maximum flow. A 2011 system called Bazaar first used it against fake accounts in online marketplaces.

Two independent shared customers are two pieces of evidence, not one shared. Give each its own copy of the buyer’s edge and add the paths: $4 + $1 = $5. The paper calls this summation flow.

The second customer’s copy is mostly wasted, while the first could carry $8. Pool the copies into one edge worth 2 × $4 and let the strongest path draw on it: the candidate scores $8. The paper calls this amplified flow.

Side by side: the candidate scores $4, $5 and $8 under maximum, summation and amplified flow. The attacker scores three cents under all three, the cents its fakes gave to real providers.

The paper proves it: however many fakes it creates, a score never exceeds the money the attacker gave away.

Results

The test: 157 days of real payments by AI agents through x402, the most used way for agents to pay for web requests. 66 million payments, worth $6.1 million, across 16 blockchains.

Each rating read 90 days of history. Then 813 buyers each paid a provider they never had before. Was that provider the rating’s first pick?

The paper calls this utility and measures it as precision at 1, written P@1: the share of the 813 buyers whose first pick was right.

Co-user count, the standard recommendation rule, gets 23.2%. Amplified flow gets 22.8%. Usage ranking, today’s marketplace rule, gets 3.7%. Maximum flow ties so often that it rarely names a single first pick.

The paper calls the second measure attack cost: what an attacker pays to be first pick for all 813 buyers, who spent $41,331 between them. Co-user volume: two cents. Co-user count: $358. Amplified flow: $8,259, a fifth of everything those buyers spent and 23 times the standard rule’s price.

Usage ranking looks expensive at $217,539, but that is 12.8 million fees at two cents each, the fakes needed to out-count the busiest real providers. On a cheaper chain the price falls with the fee, and the rating is right for 3.7% of buyers.

Both measures at once, as the paper draws them. A rating belongs in the top right: useful, and costly to fake. Anduin’s summation and amplified flow are the only two there.

What the paper shows

Payments already on the ledger can rate providers. Nothing about the market has to change.

A fake buyer earns trust only by paying providers the buyer already pays, so every point of rating costs real money.

Under amplified flow, becoming every buyer’s first pick costs a fifth of what those buyers spent, 23 times the price under the standard recommendation rule.

Data: 66,073,201 x402 payments across 16 blockchains, March 23 to August 26, 2026. Ratings used the 90 days before June 21, 2026; the 813 buyers chose in the 30 days after. Chart values include each payment’s fee.
